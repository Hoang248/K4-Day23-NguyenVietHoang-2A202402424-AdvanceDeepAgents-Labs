# World Models: From Latent Predictors to Interactive Simulators

## TL;DR
- World models span learned predictive representations and generative simulators: early systems compressed observations and predicted uncertain latent futures, while later agents use imagined rollouts for behavior learning and planning. [1][2][3]
- Latent dynamics can make planning more computationally tractable than repeatedly predicting full-resolution video, but learned representations must preserve task-relevant factors and remain robust to visual distractors. [4][5]
- A newer line turns video generation into action-conditioned environments, using latent actions, explicit control tokens, or action modules; these approaches trade interaction granularity and speed against temporal consistency and fidelity. [6][7][8][9][10]
- World models have been used both for imagined policy learning and real-robot control; results support promise in selected tasks, not universal transfer or reliable long-horizon prediction. [3][5][11]

## Background
A world model is an internal predictive model of how observations or latent states evolve, typically conditioned on actions, that an agent can use to anticipate outcomes rather than relying only on direct interaction. Early work combined a compressed visual representation with recurrent temporal prediction; importantly, the predictor represented uncertain outcomes as a distribution rather than a single deterministic next state. [1][2] This matters because model-based agents can use predicted futures as an internal environment for policy learning or action selection, while the same basic idea now appears in video generation, robotics, and autonomous-driving simulation. [3][5][7]

## Latent dynamics and task-relevant representations
One influential design separates visual compression from temporal dynamics: an encoder maps observations to a compact latent state, and a recurrent model predicts future latent states conditioned on history and action. This can support imagined interaction without rendering every predicted frame, as in the early World Models formulation and later latent-dynamics agents. [1][2][3] DreamerV3 learns a model and improves behavior by imagining future scenarios; its authors report a single configuration across more than 150 tasks, an author-reported breadth that is not evidence of arbitrary deployment robustness. [3]

Representation quality is a central trade-off. Task-relevant latent models seek to discard irrelevant visual variation, but overly compressed state can also omit cues needed for control. The retrieved HF discovery summary describes Learning Latent Dynamic Robust Representations as targeting exogenous visual distractors through spatiotemporal masking, bisimulation, latent reconstruction, and a hybrid recurrent state-space model, and reports gains on visually complex control tasks; it does not provide enough detail to independently assess effect size or generalization. [4] Physical-robot learning illustrates why the representation/dynamics distinction matters operationally: DayDreamer trains on replayed real experience and learns behaviors through latent imagined rollouts, while its paper notes the computational expense and drift risks of pixel/video prediction. [5]

## World models as planners and robot-learning simulators
In model-based reinforcement learning, learned dynamics support two related uses: generating imagined experience for policy improvement, and evaluating candidate actions before committing to them. DreamerV3 exemplifies policy learning from imagined futures, whereas DayDreamer applies model-based learning directly to physical robot experience and reports results in locomotion, visual pick-and-place, and navigation. [3][5] These results show that latent simulation can be useful beyond a conventional external physics simulator, but the robot evidence covers a limited set of setups and does not establish broad cross-embodiment transfer. [5]

Video-token models offer another bridge between prediction and control. iVideoGPT autoregressively models visual observations, actions, and rewards in one token sequence, with intermediate control intended to retain stepwise interaction; the paper reports simulated visual-control comparisons and explicitly identifies limited robot-data diversity and a lack of complex real-robot evaluation. [12] V-JEPA 2 takes a different route: it first pretrains an action-free joint-embedding-predictive model, then post-trains an action-conditioned model, V-JEPA 2-AC, for robotic planning. [11] This two-stage design does not by itself establish broad transfer across robot types. Taken together, these approaches expose a core design choice: optimize predictive representations for scalable pretraining, or tightly couple them to action-conditioned control and task utility. [5][11][12]

## From video prediction to controllable interactive worlds
Interactive video simulators turn predicted frames into observations an agent can influence. Genie builds an environment from unlabeled video using a spatiotemporal tokenizer, a learned latent-action model, and autoregressive frame-token dynamics; latent actions permit intervention without ground-truth action labels, but their meaning is less directly interpretable than familiar controls, and the principal demonstrated setting is 2D platform games. [6] GAIA-1 instead conditions next-token image prediction on past image, text, and driving-action tokens, then uses a video decoder to render higher-resolution video; its authors report that autoregressive generation was not yet real-time. [7]

Other systems emphasize generation speed or reusable pretrained video models. Oasis uses a spatial autoencoder and latent-diffusion backbone, generates frames autoregressively conditioned on user inputs, and its project page reports real-time video generation. [8] Dynamic World Simulation adapts pretrained video generators with an action-conditioning module and a motion-reinforced loss intended to prioritize changes caused by actions; the paper reports applications in games and robotics, while the retrieved conclusion excerpt attributes limitations to modeling videos with extended temporal horizons or high spatial resolutions. [9] These projects differ in whether they learn an interactive model from video or adapt a generative prior, yet both confront the same problem: visually plausible continuation is not by itself proof that the simulated dynamics respond correctly to actions. [6][8][9]

## Control interfaces, interactivity, and evaluation
Action representation affects how useful a generated world is. Genie infers discrete latent actions from video, GAIA-1 uses explicit driving and text tokens, and Oasis conditions on user inputs; these choices determine whether a model offers semantic controls, learned interventions, or a domain-specific interface. [6][7][8] iVideoGPT's multimodal sequence and MineWorld's interleaved image/action tokens instead foreground stepwise prediction and intervention; MineWorld reports parallel decoding and Minecraft frame-rate results, but its evidence remains domain-specific rather than demonstrating general-purpose simulation. [10][12]

Evaluation therefore needs to separate image quality, action-following, and downstream task utility. The retrieved work reports different targets—visual-control benchmarks, robot planning, game simulation, or video consistency—so comparisons across papers cannot be read as a single leaderboard. [3][5][8][11][10][12] In particular, high visual fidelity does not establish accurate counterfactual outcomes, and action-conditioned response does not establish persistent object/state consistency; reported constraints in Genie, GAIA-1, and MineWorld illustrate why functional and perceptual evaluation both matter. [6][7][10]

## Trends and open problems
Recent work is moving from compact, task-trained latent dynamics toward pretrained video representations and larger action-conditioned video simulators, with hybrids that post-train action-conditioned predictors or add action modules to pretrained generators. [9][11][12] This broadening may improve coverage, but the retrieved evidence does not establish that general-purpose video pretraining reliably yields precise control; the limitations reported across sources include long-horizon drift, limited context, narrow domains, inference cost, and scarce robot data. [5][6][8][9][11][12]

A second trend is closer integration of prediction with planning: world models are increasingly evaluated as components in model-predictive control, imagined policy learning, or interactive rollouts, rather than only as video predictors. [3][5][11][12] Open problems include preserving physical and action consistency over long horizons, constructing representations that retain geometry and task-relevant state while ignoring distractors, making models efficient enough for closed-loop use, and testing whether simulated visual success transfers to real task performance. [4][5][7][8][9][11][10][12] Claims of broad capability remain unsettled in this evidence base: most results are reported by the originating papers or project pages, and task-specific demonstrations should not be generalized beyond their tested environments. [3][5][6][8][11][10]
