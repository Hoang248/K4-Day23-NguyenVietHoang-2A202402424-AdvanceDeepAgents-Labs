# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: Methods, Trade-offs, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- Small-model capability is not simply a parameter-count outcome: pretraining compact models, curating instructive data, staged synthetic-data training, and teacher supervision all contribute, but the reported gains are tied to particular tasks and evaluations. [1][2][3][4]
- Inference efficiency comprises distinct levers: better KV-cache allocation, quantization, faster attention kernels, and speculative decoding address different memory, bandwidth, and serial-generation costs rather than providing interchangeable speedups. [5][6][7][8][9]
- Training recipes are evolving toward data- and scale-aware experimentation: small-run data selection can predict larger-run rankings within a studied suite, while continued pretraining can exhibit an initial stability gap and may require careful data mixtures. [10][11][12]
- “Small” and “efficient” are deployment-relative. Model quality should be assessed alongside latency, memory, energy, and hardware/workload conditions; studies of mobile applications and on-device models show that model size alone is not a sufficient deployment metric. [13][14][15][16]

## Background
Small language models (SLMs) are commonly discussed as models that can fit tighter compute, memory, power, or latency budgets, but parameter thresholds vary with device capability and time. One benchmark survey scopes decoder-only SLMs to 100M–5B parameters and explicitly notes that “small” can drift as device memory changes. [13] Efficient inference addresses both the cost of generating tokens and the resource cost of storing model weights and intermediate state; small-model research additionally asks how far data quality, training strategy, and specialization can compensate for reduced parameter count. [5][6][1][2] The practical motivation is to make model use viable in constrained or local settings without treating capability, responsiveness, privacy, or operating cost as a single objective. [14][17]

## Building capability into compact models: data, pretraining, and supervision
A compact model’s training data and supervision strategy can matter as much as a nominal size comparison suggests. Foundational compact-model experiments found pretraining important even for small architectures, with pretrained distillation improving on the baseline; the work is on compact representation models, so it is a precedent rather than direct proof for generative SLMs. [1] For code generation, Phi-1’s “textbook” recipe filtered instructional material and added a synthetic exercise fine-tuning stage: the 1.3B model reports HumanEval pass@1 of 50.6%, while the authors say exercise fine-tuning is crucial and note a 29% score before that stage. [2] Phi-1.5 extends the synthetic textbook-data approach to natural-language tasks and reports some capabilities comparable to models five times larger, but also documents hallucination and uneven benchmark performance. [3]

Other recipes emphasize scale and training schedules rather than relying on synthetic data alone. TinyLlama’s abstract describes the 1.1B v1.0 model as pretrained on around one trillion tokens for up to three epochs, without clarifying there whether this is corpus size or cumulative exposure; it separately says v1.1 was trained for two trillion tokens. [18] MiniCPM uses extensive “wind tunnel” experiments to tune training and reports 1.2B and 2.4B non-embedding-parameter models with capabilities characterized by its authors as comparable to 7B–13B systems. [10]

Distillation can transfer more than answer labels, but its success depends on what is transferred and on student/teacher fit. Distilling Step-by-Step reports that adding teacher-generated rationales helped smaller models across 4 NLP benchmarks, including a result where a fine-tuned 770M T5 outperformed few-shot 540B PaLM using 80% of the dataset; these findings concern the reported tasks and rationale-supervision setup. [4] Conversely, the capacity-gap study argues that a larger teacher is not always better for a given student, reporting an approximately linear relationship between tested student scale and optimal teacher scale in its experiments. [19] Together these results argue against assuming one distillation recipe or teacher size is best for every SLM.

## Inference mechanisms: memory, precision, and autoregressive decoding
Serving improvements target different bottlenecks. PagedAttention manages KV-cache memory in non-contiguous blocks, reducing fragmentation and over-reservation through on-demand allocation and sharing. In the paper’s tested serving setups, vLLM reports 2–4× throughput over FasterTransformer and Orca at the same latency, with larger gains in some longer-sequence and more complex-decoding cases; this is a workload-specific systems result, not a model-independent multiplier. [5] KIVI instead compresses the cache asymmetrically—keys per channel and values per token—and reports 2.6× lower peak memory and 2.35–3.47× throughput on evaluated workloads. The paper also reports model-dependent accuracy risk, including a case where its tested Falcon-7B setup may need 4-bit rather than 2-bit quantization. [6]

Weight quantization is a separate lever from KV-cache compression. GPTQ applies one-shot, weight-only post-training quantization; its reported 3–4-bit results show how lower-precision weights can reduce memory movement, while the paper explicitly says this does not itself reduce the computation count. Thus low-bit weights may help memory-bound inference, but they do not guarantee a speedup independent of kernels, hardware, or model/task accuracy. [7] Kernel-level attention work tackles a different cost: FlashAttention-2 changes tiling and work partitioning to reduce memory traffic and improve GPU utilization, using exact rather than approximate attention. Its highlighted speed measurements are primarily attention-kernel/training results, so they should not be read as an equivalent end-to-end autoregressive decoding gain. [9]

Speculative decoding attacks serial token generation by having a faster draft model propose multiple tokens for parallel verification by the target model. The cited speculative-sampling method reports preservation of the target output distribution and a 2–2.5× decoding speedup in its Chinchilla 70B experiment; gains depend on draft/target agreement and the cost of drafting and verification. [8] This differs fundamentally from quantization or cache management: it seeks fewer sequential target-model steps, whereas those methods reduce per-step resource demands. [6][7][8]

## Training and adaptation as compute allocation
Training a small model well includes deciding what data to use and how to adapt it. DataDecide evaluates pretraining data comparisons across 25 corpora, models up to 1B parameters, and multiple seeds; within that suite it reports that small experiments can predict target-scale corpus rankings, with a 150M model achieving about 80% pairwise ranking accuracy for a 1B target. The finding supports small-run screening as a practical aid, but does not establish that data rankings always transfer across domains or model families. [11]

Continued pretraining can improve domain capability without training from scratch, but adaptation is not necessarily monotonic. The stability-gap study reports an initial decline in domain-task performance followed by recovery and warns of potential general-task forgetting. In its medical-domain OpenLlama-3B tests, a high-quality subset repeated over multiple epochs improved the reported medical average from 36.2% to 40.7% using 40% of the original training budget; the authors report no forgetting on their measured general tasks under those conditions. [12] MiniCPM’s schedule designed for continued training and TinyLlama’s domain-oriented stages illustrate the broader shift from one-shot pretraining toward staged, domain-aware training. [18][10] A remaining limitation is that these outcomes are tied to chosen data mixtures, learning rates, evaluation domains, and tested model families; measured retention on selected tasks is not evidence of universal knowledge retention. [10][12]

## Evaluation and deployment: measure the system, not just the model
Evaluation should jointly report capability and resource use. A survey and measurement study of SLMs benchmarks capability domains alongside inference latency and memory footprints, while noting that the “small” label itself shifts with available devices. [13] A broader edge-deployment study covers more than 60 public SLMs and identifies limited in-context learning as a continuing capability constraint; its proposed directions include task-specific routing and model/hardware co-design. [20] These broad comparisons help map the design space, but do not replace workload-specific device measurements.

Application-level tests can reverse conclusions drawn from model size or isolated throughput. In one smartphone-sensor application study, only models under 4B parameters ran successfully on the tested powerful phones; meaningful-output mobile latency exceeded 30 seconds while cloud latency was under 10 seconds, and compression could reduce hardware demands while substantially degrading performance. Those results describe the tested task and systems, not universal mobile/cloud latency limits. [14] A separate on-device evaluation across models, quantization methods, and commodity hardware reports that heavily quantized larger models can outperform smaller higher-precision models in its tested setting, with a threshold near 3.5 effective bits per weight; it also reports that the throughput bottleneck shifts with model size. [15]

Recent evaluation work increasingly treats energy and reproducibility as first-class metrics. SLM-Bench describes a standardized evaluation across 15 SLMs, nine NLP tasks, 23 datasets, 14 domains, and four hardware configurations, quantifying correctness, computation, and consumption; breadth of coverage is useful but does not imply that every deployment workload is represented. [16] Edge-first analysis likewise frames placement as a trade-off among quality, responsiveness, energy, and cost, and notes that cloud fallback may remain necessary when edge capacity or model capability is insufficient. [17] Consequently, fair comparisons need to state model/version, precision, prompt and output lengths, batch/concurrency, device and runtime, task-quality measure, and whether measurements cover prefill, token generation, and sustained operation. This is a methodological recommendation motivated by the workload- and hardware-specific differences reported across these studies. [5][6][14][15][16][17]

## Trends and open problems
The field is moving from parameter-count competition toward co-optimization of data, training, inference, and hardware. Compact-model work combines synthetic or curated data with pretraining and targeted supervision, while recent studies test data-selection proxies and staged domain adaptation. [2][3][10][11][12] At inference time, the trend is compositional: KV-cache allocation and compression, weight quantization, attention kernels, and speculative decoding each address a different bottleneck and may be combined, but the resulting quality and speed depend on workload and implementation. [5][6][7][8][9]

Several problems remain unresolved. First, cross-paper performance comparisons remain fragile because studies use different models, tasks, hardware, baselines, and metrics; the mobile latency figures, quantization threshold, and serving throughput gains are specific study results rather than universal constants. [5][14][15] Second, capability gaps remain: broad SLM analysis reports limited in-context learning, and specialized benchmark gains do not demonstrate robust general reasoning or reliable behavior in deployment. [3][20] Third, continued pretraining and aggressive compression require more systematic assessment of forgetting and quality trade-offs beyond each paper’s tested conditions. [6][12][15] Finally, edge/cloud routing and energy-aware measurement are promising but require transparent, reproducible workload definitions; a model that is attractive in parameter count may still be slower, less capable, or less efficient on a particular device. [14][16][17]
