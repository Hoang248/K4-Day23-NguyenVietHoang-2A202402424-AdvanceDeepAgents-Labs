# Video and Multimodal Generation: Architectures, Control, and Open Challenges

## TL;DR
- Video generation has moved from extending image diffusion into fixed-length clips toward compressed latent representations and transformer-based or autoregressive sequence models; each family trades global temporal modeling against sequence cost and sampling flexibility. [1][2][3][4]
- Long-form generation is being approached through temporal extension, memory, or hybrid autoregressive-plus-diffusion designs, but a claimed long horizon is not by itself evidence of stable coherence over arbitrary durations. [2][5][6][7]
- Multimodal conditioning ranges from shared-token models and semantic adapters to explicit motion tracks and audio-derived scene layouts; these designs offer distinct forms of control rather than one established universal architecture. [4][8][9][10][11][12]
- Evaluation is increasingly decomposed into temporal quality, motion, and transition-following, because smooth-looking or prompt-aligned frames alone can conceal missing actions or weak dynamics. [13][14]
- Interactive video models make action-conditioned streaming a concrete research direction, but reported throughput and fidelity remain bounded by particular model, hardware, task, and evaluation settings. [15]

## Background
Video generation learns to synthesize temporally ordered visual content, commonly conditioned on text, images, motion, or other signals. It matters because high-quality generation requires not only plausible individual frames but also coherent change over time and reliable response to the conditioning input. Early diffusion work adapted image-generation models to video using space-time architectures and autoregressive extension; subsequent systems have expanded the design space to latent video diffusion, token-based sequence generation, and multimodal control. [1][2][3]

## From image diffusion to video-native latent and token models
An early diffusion approach uses a 3D U-Net, jointly trains on images and videos, and generates a fixed number of frames; its longer-video strategy applies the model autoregressively using conditional generation. This makes video an extension of image diffusion, but does not remove the challenge of extending a finite clip. [1] Imagen Video illustrates a complementary cascade: a text-conditioned base video model is followed by interleaved spatial and temporal super-resolution models, with progressive distillation proposed to accelerate sampling. [16]

Later systems shift where and how video structure is represented. Stable Video Diffusion encodes video into a latent space and builds from image diffusion through temporal mixing layers and staged training, including video pretraining and high-quality finetuning. [3] Phenaki instead compresses video into discrete tokens with causal temporal attention and predicts them using a text-conditioned masked transformer; its causal tokenizer supports variable-length output and changing prompts over time. [2] VideoPoet takes a decoder-only autoregressive route over discrete multimodal tokens, adapting one pretrained model to video tasks including text-to-video, image-to-video, editing, and stylization. [4]

These approaches expose a recurring architectural trade-off rather than a settled winner. Latent diffusion inherits useful image-model components and avoids operating the main denoiser on raw pixels, while token autoregression offers a natural sequential interface for variable-duration generation and multimodal task adaptation. Yet token sequences become costly at high resolution, and autoregressive systems must manage long-range dependencies; diffusion models also face expensive spatiotemporal computation. [2][3][4] Lumiere addresses temporal structure differently: a Space-Time U-Net generates the full temporal span in one pass rather than synthesizing sparse keyframes and filling the gaps through a separate temporal cascade. This aims to improve global coherence, while still using multiscale processing and spatial upscaling rather than demonstrating unrestricted duration. [5]

## Extending duration: windows, memory, and hybrid generation
Fixed-span generation has prompted several distinct continuation strategies. Phenaki uses causal video tokens and autoregressive extension to support variable-length stories. [2] Progressive windowing and memory-augmented generation offer other ways to retain context: MALT generates short segments with a diffusion transformer and recurrent temporal attention, carrying compact hidden-state memory from earlier segments into later ones. [7] Such designs aim to preserve more history than a narrow preceding-frame window, but remain segment-based and do not establish indefinitely stable real-world coherence. [7]

Hybrid systems divide labor between coarse long-range planning and detailed synthesis. ARLON first predicts coarse, long-range discrete visual tokens autoregressively, then conditions a diffusion transformer to render high-quality segments; its authors report benchmark gains against their selected baseline, while the evidence remains tied to the paper's setup and metrics. [6] Lumiere's full-span generation presents a contrasting strategy: model the temporal span together rather than assemble it through sequential segments. [5] Taken together, these papers suggest a design tension: sequential or memory-based methods facilitate continuation, while joint temporal modeling targets coherence across a span; neither reported approach eliminates compute, error accumulation, or the need to assess quality over longer horizons. [5][6][7]

## Multimodal inputs and structured control
Text is only one way to specify a video. VideoPoet treats image, video, audio, and text as tokenized modalities in an autoregressive model, while Omni-Video connects an autoregressive multimodal language-model branch to a pretrained diffusion decoder through an adapter that maps semantic visual cues into the decoder's conditioning space. These illustrate, respectively, shared token modeling and a bridge between semantic reasoning and a specialized video synthesizer. [4][10]

Other systems expose more structured control. MotionCtrl separates camera motion from object motion, using camera-pose sequences to control camera motion and object trajectories to control object motion. [9] Motion Prompting conditions video on sparse or dense spatiotemporal tracks and demonstrates camera/object motion control, motion transfer, and image editing. [8] Earlier motion-conditioned diffusion work first completes sparse user-drawn flow into denser motion before synthesizing future frames, illustrating how an intermediate representation can address underspecified controls. [12]

Audio-conditioned approaches likewise differ in where multimodality enters the pipeline. SpA2V first asks a multimodal planner to infer a video scene layout from spatial audio cues, then uses the layout to guide a pretrained video-generation pipeline. [11] This planning-plus-synthesis approach contrasts with systems that jointly model audio and video tokens or latents; the retrieved sources describe differing architectures, but do not establish a common-compute comparison or a generally superior strategy. [4][10][11] Across both spatial and audio control, explicit conditions can make requests more actionable, but they introduce representation and alignment dependencies: tracks must be specified or inferred, and errors in an intermediate plan can propagate into generated frames. [8][11][12]

## Evaluation: coherence, motion, and temporal compositionality
Evaluation needs to separate properties that a single overall score can obscure. VBench decomposes video-generation quality into specific, hierarchical, and disentangled dimensions, including motion smoothness and temporal flickering, rather than treating quality as one undifferentiated target. [13][17]

Transition-sensitive evaluation asks a different question: did the requested event or state change actually happen? TC-Bench tests temporal compositionality by specifying transitions such as attribute changes, object-relation changes, or background shifts, and proposes assertion-based checks for transition completion and consistency. Its authors report that common similarity-based measures can fail to capture such transitions, and that consistency alone does not imply successful completion. [14] Motion Prompting's trajectory error and appearance measures add a more direct assessment of whether generated tracks follow specified motion, but such adherence measures do not by themselves establish realism or broad long-horizon coherence. [8]

The broader implication is that evaluation should match the control and use case: frame-level appearance, temporal stability, dynamic range, event completion, and condition adherence are related but non-interchangeable. [13][14][8] The retrieved benchmarks also leave open how robustly automated metrics transfer across domains and how well they capture physical plausibility, semantic correctness, or extended temporal drift. [13][14]

## Interactive generation and world-model ambitions
Action-conditioned streaming extends generation from producing a fixed clip toward responding as a user acts. The Matrix describes streaming generation and reports 720p video at up to 16 FPS; the abstract describes limited supervised data from videogames complemented by large-scale unsupervised footage from real-world settings. These are author-reported results in a particular setup, not an independent service-level measurement. [15]

Because generation is streaming, the system is closer to sequential interaction than a fixed-clip generator. Its reported throughput alone, however, does not establish fidelity as a general-purpose simulator. [15]

## Trends and open problems
The trajectory across the retrieved work is toward video-native representations, longer temporal context, multimodal conditioning, and interactive action loops. [2][4][6][7][10][15] Architectures increasingly combine components—latent encoders, autoregressive context, diffusion refinement, memory, or adapters—rather than relying on a single unmodified image-generation model. [3][6][7][10]

Important problems remain unresolved. Longer-duration generation raises questions about cumulative error and memory, while efficient high-resolution generation remains constrained by the cost of video tokens or repeated denoising. [2][4][6][7] Control and multimodal synchronization depend on accurate conditions or intermediate representations, and the retrieved work does not establish robust generalization across arbitrary scenes and inputs. [8][9][11] Finally, benchmarks reveal that temporal consistency, motion, transition completion, and visual quality can diverge; evaluation needs to report these separately and better test physical plausibility and long-horizon behavior. [13][14][8][15] Reported system comparisons are therefore best read as results within specified datasets, metrics, and hardware settings, not as a universal ranking of video-generation approaches. [6][13][15]
